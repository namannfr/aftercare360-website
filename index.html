Connected recovery infrastructure · UK digital health

Recovery continuity, connected across services.

Aftercare 360 is developing a consent-led digital platform that links mental-health and substance-use services, keeps recovery progress visible between appointments, and stays useful after someone leaves treatment.

Designed for local-authority commissioners, NHS and ICB teams, and treatment providers, with the person in recovery in control of what is shared.

The challenge

Two systems, one person.

For most adults in drug and alcohol treatment in England, mental-health needs are part of the picture. Yet mental-health care and substance-use treatment are funded, delivered and measured as separate systems.

PROBLEM 1

Fragmented services

Mental-health care is mostly delivered through the NHS. Drug and alcohol treatment is commissioned by local-authority public health and usually delivered by third-sector providers. Records and assessments rarely travel between them, so people repeat their story at each hand-off. National guidance has called for joined-up care for co-occurring conditions since at least 2017.

PROBLEM 2

Recovery measured periodically

Recovery capital, the personal, social and community resources that sustain recovery, is usually captured by questionnaire at entry, review and exit. Between those points, change is hard to see until it shows up as a crisis or a return to treatment.

PROBLEM 3

Loss of visibility after discharge

Contact and measurement usually end when treatment ends. Once someone leaves a service, whether through a planned completion or not, it becomes much harder for anyone to see how they are doing or to offer support early.

Our platform

A shared, consented layer across the service boundary.

Aftercare 360 is proposed as connected recovery infrastructure: one recovery picture that both sides of the boundary can see, with the person deciding what is shared, and a recovery measure that keeps working between appointments and after discharge.

Pillar 1

One consented recovery record

A connected view of a person's recovery journey across the services they use, governed by their permissions and by information-governance agreements between organisations.

Responds to: fragmented services

Pillar 2

Continuous recovery-capital measurement

The Dynamic Digital Recovery Capital Index: a proposed measure that updates recovery capital from short check-ins, service events and practitioner contacts, instead of only at scheduled reviews. uses short check-ins, service events and practitioner contacts to track change over time.

Responds to: periodic measurement

Pillar 3

Continuity beyond discharge

The person keeps their recovery picture and support connections after treatment ends. If their signal declines, an authorised practitioner is prompted to consider contact.

Responds to: lost visibility after discharge

Pillar 4

Cross-service outcomes visibility

Commissioners see aggregated, cohort-level measures of recovery-capital change, engagement after discharge and coordination between services.

Responds to: outcomes held in single-service returns

Designed to complement, not replace. Aftercare 360 is intended to connect to existing NHS, local-authority and provider systems. It is not a clinical record, a case-management system or a treatment intervention in its own right.

Three views, one platform

Each person sees what their role needs.

Keep hold of your own progress

For the person in recovery, Aftercare 360 is intended to be quick, private and useful to them first. It is always free to use, and nothing is shared without their say.

  • Simple check-ins. A few taps, about a minute a week, on a phone.
  • Personal goals. Goals they set, with progress they can see.
  • Recovery-capital trend. Strengths shown across personal, social, community and cultural domains.
  • Granular consent. Choose which service sees what, change it at any time, and see who has looked.
  • Support contacts. Named contacts and relevant local resources, kept after discharge.

How it works

From consent to human follow-up, in five stages.

The sequence below shows how information is intended to move through the platform. Select a stage for more detail.

The core measure

Dynamic Digital Recovery Capital Index (DDRCI)

Recovery capital describes the personal, social, community and cultural resources a person can draw on to sustain recovery. It is a well-established idea in UK and international research, and validated questionnaires exist to measure it.

The DDRCI is proposed as a way to turn that periodic score into a continuously updated signal, anchored to an established brief instrument and refreshed by short check-ins and service events between reviews.

How the index is intended to work
  1. Anchor. A validated brief recovery-capital instrument at baseline and periodically calibrates each person's score.
  2. Micro check-ins. Three to five rotating questions, about a minute a week, mapped to four domains.
  3. Event signals. Attended or missed appointments, referrals, and housing or employment changes recorded by services.
  4. Fusion. A statistical model updates a score per domain, with a total, a rate of change, and a confidence band that widens when data are sparse.
  5. Outputs. A strengths view for the person, a prompt to the practitioner when change crosses an agreed threshold, and anonymised trends for commissioners.

Validation against established recovery-capital measures is planned in Phase 1. The index has not yet been built or validated.

What the DDRCI is not

  • It does not diagnose any condition.
  • It does not predict an individual's clinical risk.
  • It does not make decisions. Prompts go to a person, who decides.
Innovation

The change is in what is measured, when, and across which boundary.

Aftercare 360 is not proposed as another recovery app. Its distinctive elements sit in measurement, workflow and governance. All five core innovations are at concept stage; their value still has to be shown in practice.

Longer-term opportunities

Planned extensions, each subject to evidence from earlier phases.

Planned · Phase 2

Community recovery assets

Map local mutual-aid groups, housing, employment, education, faith and cultural organisations, and match them to a person's weakest recovery-capital domain, with a warm introduction from a peer worker.

Planned · Phase 2

Peer recovery coordination

Give peer workers with lived experience a task queue from reach-out prompts and linkage matches, and involve lived-experience panels in designing check-in questions.

Planned · Phase 2 to 3

Family and carer involvement

Let the person nominate supporters and choose exactly what they can see, such as “check-in missed” but never clinical detail, with guidance for supporters.

Planned · Phase 2

Cross-service value measurement

Report cohort trajectories, handover timeliness and post-discharge engagement against each area's own baseline, mapped to national dataset fields to avoid double entry.

Subject to validation · Phase 3

Recovery data trust

Consented, de-identified data held in a trusted research environment that approved researchers can apply to use, with outputs checked before release.

Gated · Phase 3

Explainable early warning

Research only until multi-area validation, bias audit, an MHRA classification decision and a clinical-safety case are complete. A practitioner would always see the reasons and make the decision.

Trust, privacy and safety

Accountability built into the design from the start.

Mental-health and substance-use information is among the most sensitive data a person has. Privacy cannot be guaranteed by design alone, so Aftercare 360 treats it as a set of commitments that must be built, assured and independently checked.

Person-controlled, granular consent

Sharing set by data type, organisation and purpose. Time-limited grants, one-tap withdrawal and a receipt for every change.

Data minimisation and purpose limitation

Collect only what a named purpose needs, and use it only for that purpose. Commissioners see aggregated data, not individuals.

Role-based access

What a practitioner, peer worker or commissioner can see depends on their role and on the person's consent, enforced where data is accessed.

Auditable access and sharing

Every view, export and sharing decision is logged. People can read their own access log: who viewed what, and when.

Special-category safeguards

A documented UK GDPR Article 9 basis, a data protection impact assessment for each area, data-sharing agreements and UK-region hosting.

Human oversight

Prompts carry plain-English reasons and lead to human contact. No automated decisions about a person's care or access to services.

Clinical-safety management

Clinical risk management to DCB0129 as a supplier, with support for buyers' own DCB0160 obligations.

NHS and public-sector assurance

Built to meet the Data Security and Protection Toolkit (DSPT) and the Digital Technology Assessment Criteria (DTAC). These are requirements still to be met.

Development roadmap

Prove the core in one area before adding anything riskier.

The build is deliberately sequenced. Each phase has a gate, and higher-risk capabilities wait until the evidence, data volume and clinical-safety work are in place. Nothing below is live today.

Market and business model

A defined buyer with a protected budget line.

In England, local authorities commission drug and alcohol treatment from the Public Health Grant. From April 2026, drug and alcohol funding is consolidated into that grant with a service-specific ring-fence, on a multi-year allocation. That makes local-authority public health the natural first buyer.

Need remains a national priority: 5,565 drug-poisoning deaths were registered in England and Wales in 2024, the highest number on record (ONS). Sources for the funding position: Health Business, 2026.

Market sizing, England

Values use a planning assumption of £96 per active person per year and exclude implementation fees. The SOM is a target, not a forecast of contracted business, and pricing still has to be validated with commissioners.

How the business is intended to earn

Commissioner-funded software contracts. Free for the person in recovery, always.

Implementation and integration fee

£30,000 per area*

One-off, covering integration with provider and NHS systems, data protection and information-governance support, configuration, training and go-live.

Annual platform licence

£96 per active person*

Priced per active person rather than per seat, so cost tracks the cohort actually supported. Inactive records are not charged.

Outcome-linked element

Under consideration

A small, automatic share linked to two or three agreed measures, designed never to become a barrier to access.

Programme-level licences

Future

Area-wide or ICB-wide agreements as adoption grows, with NHS mental-health services as potential co-buyers. One person, one licence: never charged twice.

Research access

Future, gated

Cost-recovery access to de-identified data through a trusted research environment, only with consent, ethics and governance in place.
